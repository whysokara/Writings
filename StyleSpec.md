**Length:** median 3 words, mean ≈ 2.6. Single-word titles are the single largest bucket: **10/29 (34%)** — *Amoeba, Autos, Chess, Commit, Comparatively, Cry, Glitch, Life, Office, ROI*. These are ALWAYS either a repurposed tech/finance term or a bare abstract noun.

**Grammatical form:** noun phrase dominates, **~69% (20/29)**. Full-sentence titles are SOMETIMES (~17%, e.g. *And then you smile*, *Universe has a CMO now*). Question-form titles are RARE (1/29: *What does a men want*). Never an imperative title.

**Capitalization:** Title Case is USUAL for compound titles but broken deliberately often enough to be a real pattern, not noise: sentence-case with only the first word capped (*And then you smile*, *I want what diwali has*, *Old new tshirt*), and one likely-unintentional internal-cap typo left uncorrected (*India World CUp*) — treat this as evidence the author does **not** copyedit titles, not as a device to imitate on purpose.

**Titles never state the thesis outright** — they name the *vehicle* (the metaphor's object), not the *tenor* (what it's actually about). "Commit" is about feeling stuck in a personal rut, not git. "Chess" is about class immobility, not the game. "Amoeba" is about life's shapelessness, and the word "amoeba" itself doesn't appear until line 12, after a full paragraph of denial ("Life is a circle? / No dude."). This delayed/oblique relationship between title and text is USUALLY true (title word doesn't recur as a flat restatement until deep in the piece) rather than the title being glossed in sentence one.

**Punctuation/subtitles:** colons in titles NEVER occur. One hyphenated subtitle exists — *Haircut- Baal Baal Bache* (no space before the hyphen, Hindi phrase as the "subtitle," itself untranslated) — RARE-BUT-CHARACTERISTIC, the only instance of a title working bilingually.

**Diction:** concrete, everyday nouns (spoon, tshirt, haircut, auto, office) far outweigh abstract nouns (life, comparatively) — roughly 20 concrete-object/event titles to 9 abstract-noun titles.

**A load-bearing "error":** the title *What does a men want* uses ungrammatical "a men," while the identical sentence *inside* the piece ("So if you ever wonder what a man wants?") uses the correct "a man." The error lives only in the title — read this as a transliteration reflex from the Hindi "ek aadmi kya chahta hai," not a typo to normalize.

## SECTION 2 — ARCHITECTURE

### Opening moves (catalogued, with frequency)
1. **Flat declarative thesis in sentence one**, title's subject named immediately — USUALLY, ~12/29. *"Autos feel like the most honest way to move through a city."* (*Autos*); *"No one remembered when the board first appeared."* (*Chess*).
2. **Second-person immersive scene**, subject arrives 2–4 sentences in — SOMETIMES, ~6/29. *"You're in a cab, stuck in traffic, staring at your watch."* (*And then you smile*); *"Every morning, you open your office laptop..."* (*Glitch*).
3. **Question immediately negated** — RARE-BUT-CHARACTERISTIC, distinctive when used. *"Life is a circle? / No dude."* (*Amoeba*); *"What does a man want? ek aadmi kya chahta hai?"* (*What does a men want*).
4. **Meta-naming the phenomenon before defining it**, simile-first — SOMETIMES. *'"Used to" is a bit like that party guest who overstays their welcome.'* (*Used to*); *"Finding something genuinely perfect feels a lot like searching for a needle in a haystack..."* (*Comparatively*).
5. **Direct-address grievance/complaint** — RARE. *"Don't you all think we need a standard spoon by now?"* (*C-Type for spoons*).
6. **Cold personal anecdote, no framing** — RARE. *"A few days ago, my mama passed away."* (*What does a men want*).

The subject/title-word appears **within sentence one** in the majority of pieces (USUALLY); delayed reveal (2–3 sentences of scene/question before the actual claim lands) is a real but minority move (SOMETIMES).

### Overall shapes (frequency)
- **Extended single-metaphor, point-by-point mapping** — DOMINANT, ~16–17/29 (~57%). *Amoeba, Commit, Ctrl Alt Del, Used to, Comparatively, Lights Camera Setup, Chess, Autos, C-Type for spoons, Wired Earphone,* etc.
- **Anecdote → reflection/turn** — SOMETIMES, ~5–6/29. *And then you smile, ROI, RCB Winning IPL* (the Zippy Chippy frame), *Haircut*, *What does a men want*.
- **Catalogue/list-driven** — RARE, 3/29. *Kachra Gadi Friends* (literal bullets), *Likes and dislikes*, *Universe has a CMO now*.
- **Lineated verse/direct incantation** — RARE, 2/29. *I want what diwali has, India World CUp*.
- **Never used:** thesis→three-supporting-points→conclusion structure; numbered-steps/how-to structure; anything resembling a five-paragraph essay.

### Paragraph behaviour
Typical paragraph is 2–5 sentences. **One-sentence (or single-word) paragraphs occur regularly and always function as a hinge**, not as filler — either a pivot into the metaphor's application (*"Life?"* — *Ctrl Alt Del*) or a closing beat (*"So do we."* — *Amoeba*; *"Big mistake."* — *Haircut*). Transitions between paragraphs are **hard cuts or single-word connectives** ("But," "And," "So," "Now," "Then") rather than essayistic connective tissue — "However," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Consequently," "Nevertheless," "Therefore" **NEVER appear anywhere in the corpus.**

### Closing moves (catalogued)
1. **Echo-couplet**: restates the title's claim, compressed/inverted, as the final two lines. *"Autos are not trying to be special. / They just happen to make sense."*
2. **Open/unresolved question**: *"Everything is normal again. / Or is it?"* (*Glitch*).
3. **Callback + deferral joke**: *"Until, of course, it happens again... But that's a story for another time."* (*And then you smile*).
4. **Zoom-out generalization**: *"Maybe every part of life needs something like this."* (*Autos*); *"Tomorrow, it may look nothing like today."* (*Amoeba*).
5. **Punchline/tag button**: *"Fade to IPO."* (*Lights Camera Setup*); *"...file for emotional unemployment."* (*RCB Winning IPL*).
6. **Fourth-wall-breaking non-sequitur to a real addressee**: *"Send wallpapers 🤌🏻"* (*Ctrl Alt Del*) — RARE-BUT-CHARACTERISTIC, but important: it proves these pieces are sometimes literally addressed to one specific known person, not a general audience.
7. **Abrupt halt, no summary**: *Cry, Kachra Gadi Friends, Solo Performance* simply stop on a small observation.

**He NEVER closes with explicit summary language** ("In conclusion," "The lesson here is," "What this shows us is") — the corpus has zero instances.

### Section breaks / headers
**Never** used — no markdown headers, no "---" dividers, no numbered sections. The single formatting exception in the whole corpus is the bulleted list in *Kachra Gadi Friends*.

## SECTION 3 — SENTENCE MECHANICS

**Length and rhythm:** wide variance, not steady. Fragments as short as 2 words (*"No dude."* / *"Big mistake."* / *"So do we."*) sit directly against sentences running 35–45 words with minimal internal punctuation, especially where embedded anxious questions or Hinglish asides are stitched into a running clause without a clear boundary (*"Your mind is racing what if you miss it? What if you have to buy another ticket?"* — *And then you smile*, missing punctuation before the embedded question is characteristic, not accidental-looking in isolation but recurring enough to be a habit). **Signature rhythm: build (1–2 long, comma-run sentences) → release (one short fragment).**

**Fragments:** USUALLY present, at least one to three per piece (~70% of pieces). Jobs: (a) deliver the punch after a wind-up, (b) isolate a definitional turn. *"Life?"* / *"No dude."* / *"Big mistake."* / *"Or is it?"*

**Sentence openers:** heavy conjunction-first use — "But" (31 corpus-wide instances), "And" (19), "Maybe" (15, functioning as an adverbial opener), "So" (5). Subject-first ("You," "It," "That") also common in direct-address pieces. A recognizable device: several pieces open their very first sentence with a **"When you..." temporal subordinate clause** (*Cry*: "When you are about to cry and your eyes fill with those first tiny tears..."). **Never** opens with a literary-essay prepositional flourish ("In the annals of...," "Throughout history...").

**Clause architecture:** predominantly **paratactic** — short independent clauses stacked with periods/commas rather than nested subordination, even for complex ideas. Hypotaxis, when present, is shallow (one subordinate clause, rarely nested two deep). Parataxis intensifies in the "definition/list" pieces (*Autos, C-Type for spoons*); sentences loosen slightly in the narrative/emotional pieces (*And then you smile, Comparatively*).

**Voice:** overwhelmingly active. Passive constructions are rare (<5% of finite verbs) — *"...were rarely mentioned"* (*Chess*), *"is reduced to"* (*Comparatively*).

**Signature syntactic constructions (repeated grammatical shapes):**
1. **Anaphoric "What if" question-stacking** (2–4 in a row): *"What if you miss it? What if you have to buy another ticket? What if this messes up everything?"* (*And then you smile*); *"What if you looked again? ...What if you walked into that store...What if you tried on a watch...What if you booked a holiday..."* (*Likes and dislikes*).
2. **Negate-then-assert triplet**: *"No doors. No glass. No separation from the street."* (*Autos*); *"No AC, no fancy coffee—just chai in a flimsy cup"* (*Office*); *"Nothing fancy. Just a simple, reliable..."* (*C-Type for spoons*).

## SECTION 5 — LEXICON

**Register:** plain, mostly Anglo-Saxon, conversational-spoken register punctuated by occasional Latinate abstraction (*perception, subtleties, aesthetic, unresolved*) and constant Hinglish code-switching in the pieces that use it. Sits much closer to spoken monologue than written essay prose.

**British/Indian-English spelling — consistent and checkable:** *favourite* (*Autos*), *optimised* (*Artificial Dumbness*), *behaviour* (*Old new tshirt*), *recognising* (*ROI*), *neighbour* (*Kachra Gadi Friends*). This is ALWAYS British/Indian-English spelling, never American ("color," "optimized," "behavior" do not occur).

**Contractions:** used consistently and heavily (*you're, it's, don't, that's, doesn't*) in all prose pieces; the verse piece (*I want what diwali has*) uses fewer, favoring incantatory full forms instead.

**Signature recurring vocabulary:** *quiet/quietly* (*Cry, Kachra Gadi Friends, Chess, Autos*), *somehow* (*Amoeba, And then you smile, RCB Winning IPL*), *maybe* as a standalone sentence-opener/hedge (29 instances corpus-wide — the single most reliable fingerprint word), *genuinely* (*Comparatively, C-Type for spoons*), *honestly* (*Comparatively, India World CUp*), *yaar / kuch bhi / pata nahi kya kya* (Hinglish pieces).

**Recurring metaphor-source nouns, reused across unrelated pieces (not just the "tech" ones):** cache/cache memory, reset/factory reset, commit, Ctrl+Z, git push, RAM, bug, glitch, update, ROI, CMO, valuation, Series A — a single consistent well he returns to regardless of the piece's ostensible subject.

**Verbs:** prefers common, plain verbs (*feel, want, carry, sit, stay, move, show up*) intensified by adverbs (*"genuinely perfect," "truly chose," "quietly avoid"*) rather than reaching for rare/ornate single verbs.

**Abstraction pattern:** every piece is concrete at the opening (a cab, a scooter, a spoon, an auto-rickshaw) and pivots to abstract nouns only in the closing lines (*identity, belonging, freedom, presence, permission, connection*). This concrete→abstract movement is the structural engine of the essay, not a stray lexical habit.

**Hedges/intensifiers:** "maybe" dominates as the primary hedge, and it is placed specifically — see Section 6. "Very" appears occasionally but is not a heavy tic. "I think" as an in-line hedge is essentially absent — he asserts flatly in the body and hedges structurally, at the end, rather than qualifying individual claims.

**Jargon domains**, always **assumed, never glossed**: software/dev (git, commit, push, reset, cache, RAM, Ctrl+Z), finance/business (ROI, GST/SGST, valuation, Series A, CMO, IPO), Indian transit/daily life (auto-rickshaw, Rapido, Metro, sangeet, sutta shop), cricket (IPL, RCB, Kohli, Ranji Trophy), internet/meme culture (rizz, based takes, L+ratio, "For You" page, MrBeast).

**Regional/idiolectal markers:** consistent Roman-script Hinglish code-switching, untranslated, in **8/29 pieces (28%)** — SOMETIMES, not universal. Indian-English constructions ("I can't stress this enough," article omission, aside-clauses in Hindi mid-English-sentence).

**Profanity/intensity words:** **zero profanity anywhere in the corpus**, including the grief piece — restraint holds even at the emotional peak.

**Forbidden lexicon** (never appears; a generic LLM would reach for these by default — actively avoid): *delve, tapestry, moreover, furthermore, in conclusion, it is important to note, navigate (challenges), unlock, elevate, seamless, robust, leverage (business sense), in today's fast-paced world, at the end of the day, resonate, profound, myriad, plethora, boundless, unwavering, testament to, embark, foster, cultivate, harness the power of*, any topic-sentence formula ("This essay will discuss..."), formal transitional adverbs (*However, Nevertheless, Consequently, Thus, Hence*).

## SECTION 6 — RHETORIC AND ARGUMENT

**Assertion pattern — a real, locatable rule:** claims are stated flatly and unhedged through the body of a piece ("Autos feel like the most honest way to move through a city" — stated as fact, not "might be"), and **hedging is concentrated almost entirely in the final 1–2 lines**, via "maybe." This is a placement rule, not a general hedging tendency.

**Evidence types and rough frequency:** analogy/extended metaphor is the dominant evidentiary mode (~24/29 pieces run on it); observed/composite everyday example is the default supporting texture (~20/29 — the cab, the haircut, the sangeet dance); personal autobiographical anecdote is rarer but higher-stakes (~6–8/29 — the scooter in *ROI*, the funeral in *What does a men want*); **data/statistics never appear as real sourced data** — invented-sounding numbers are used for comic texture only (*"shrink by a solid 37%"* — *Haircut*); authority/citation is essentially absent — the closest is presenting the Zippy Chippy horse story as trivia "I once read."

**Metaphor source domains (comprehensive):** software/OS/git, consumer tech products, corporate/business/finance, biology, board games, Indian street/transit life, Bollywood/theatre framing, horse racing, party-guest personification, photography/film, retail/hospitality review culture. **Metaphors are ALWAYS clean and single** — one controlling vehicle per piece, mapped feature-by-feature, never two competing metaphors fighting inside the same piece.

**Counterargument handling:** when it appears, it's a specific, repeatable construction — raise the objection in the author's own voice, dispatch it in the same breath: *"You may argue that small restaurant owners prefer plastic spoons because washing steel spoons incurs labour charges."* → immediately rebutted. RARE-BUT-CHARACTERISTIC (fully formed in *C-Type for spoons*), never steelmanned at length.

**Humour:** dry/observational, meme-literate absurdism, occasional self-deprecation. High frequency (~20/29 pieces land at least one joke). Placement: usually the final button line, or a mid-piece parenthetical aside.

**Repetition devices:** anaphora is the strongest device in his toolkit ("What if...What if...What if"; the Diwali poem's "I see... I see..." / "To make... To make..."). Negation-triplets function as a tight tricolon variant. **Title-echo closings** — restating the title's key word verbatim in the final lines — occur in a majority of the metaphor-driven pieces (*Autos, Used to, Comparatively, C-Type for spoons*).

## SECTION 7 — VOICE AND STANCE

**Person and distribution:** second-person direct address dominates (USUALLY, ~18/29) as the observational-universalizing mode. First-person singular is reserved for genuine autobiographical disclosure (*ROI, Old new tshirt, What does a men want*). First-person plural "we" marks shared-condition social observation (*Second Hand Inflation, Office, Used to*). **The switch from "you" to "I" is itself a signal**: "you" = observational/comic mode, "I" = the piece is about to get real.

**Distance:** peer-to-peer, never lecturing — he includes himself/the reader ("we," "you") even when asserting a claim.

**Implied reader** (reconstructed from unglossed references, not stated): a young urban Indian, bilingual Hindi-English, white-collar/tech-adjacent, active on Instagram/Reels, fluent in IPL culture, startup culture, and internet meme vocabulary — evidenced by GST, Rapido, sangeet, git push, Series A, and "For You" page all being used with zero explanation.

**Self-disclosure:** low-frequency, high-intensity, and **bimodal rather than gradual** — most pieces disclose nothing personal and stay universal/observational; a small number (*ROI*'s scooter memory, *What does a men want*'s funeral scene, *Ctrl Alt Del*'s "Send wallpapers 🤌🏻") suddenly become nakedly personal, addressed to a specific real person. There is no smooth gradient between these modes.

**Confidence temperature:** structurally thinking-aloud (associative, builds live) but **always lands on a stated conclusion**, softened by "maybe" rather than left genuinely open. He has decided by the end even while performing uncertainty.

**Emotional range:** mostly light/wry/bemused. Two genuine ruptures — *What does a men want* (grief, undisguised) and *Cry*/*Kachra Gadi Friends* (quiet melancholy) — drop the joke/meme apparatus entirely, with no ironic distance. Trigger for the extreme: death and family, specifically; other serious subjects (inflation, class, identity) stay inside the comic-observational register.

## SECTION 8 — FORMATTING BEHAVIOUR

- **Lists:** rare — one true bulleted list in the whole corpus (*Kachra Gadi Friends*), no numbered lists anywhere.
- **Bold/italic:** essentially unused; one lone italicized word via asterisks (`*used to*`).
- **White space:** paragraphs separated by blank lines; the verse pieces lineate each clause on its own line for pacing/breath.

- **Currency:** ₹ (Rupees), Indian-context pricing.
- **Spelling convention:** British/Indian-English, consistently (see Section 5).

## SECTION 9 — ANTI-PATTERNS (what would immediately read as fake)

1. **Never** open with "In today's fast-paced world" or any throat-clearing generalization before the concrete subject.
2. **Never** use "However," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Nevertheless," "Thus," or "Hence" as a paragraph connective — he uses "But," "And," "So" instead.
3. **Never** close with "In conclusion" or any explicit summary statement.
4. **Never** mix two metaphor vehicles in one piece — one controlling analogy only.
5. **Never** gloss or explain the Hindi/slang/tech references — footnoting or translating them mid-sentence is an immediate tell.
6. **Never** write a five-paragraph essay with topic sentences and a restated thesis — his pieces are associative, not structured argumentation.
7. **Never** use semicolons to build a balanced compound-complex academic sentence — semicolons are nearly absent and never do that job here.
8. **Never** hedge mid-body with "I think" or "in my opinion" — assertions in the body are flat; hedging happens only structurally, at the very end, via "maybe."
9. **Never** use American spelling (color, optimized, behavior) — it's British/Indian-English throughout.
10. **Never** reach for "delve," "tapestry," "resonate," "unlock," "seamless," "leverage," "myriad," or "profound" — see the forbidden lexicon in Section 5.
11. **Never** sustain profanity or crude language, even in the grief piece.
12. **Never** add a moralizing "the lesson here is" — the piece ends on an image, a question, or an echo, not a stated takeaway.
13. **Never** decorate with more than one emoji, and never use emoji as ornamental sprinkling rather than a single closing beat.
14. **Never** cite a real study, statistic source, or named authority — invented-sounding numbers for comic texture only.

## SECTION 11 — GENERATION DIRECTIVES

*(Ordered by how badly getting them wrong would damage the imitation. Written as executable instructions.)*

1. Pick exactly **one** controlling metaphor/analogy for the whole piece before writing a word. Never introduce a second competing vehicle.
2. Draw that metaphor from one of these source domains only, matched to nothing else already overused in your recent output: software/git/OS, consumer tech, corporate/finance/startup, biology, board games, Indian street/transit life, cricket, horse racing, retail-review culture, party-guest personification.
3. State the piece's core claim **flatly, as fact**, in the body. Do not write "might be," "perhaps," or "I think" inside the body.
4. Reserve all hedging for the final one or two lines only, and hedge with "maybe" specifically — not "perhaps," "possibly," or "arguably."
5. End on one of: an echo-couplet restating the title's key word, an unresolved question, a zoom-out generalization, or an abrupt punchline. Never end with a stated moral or "the lesson here is."
6. Never write a summary sentence, a topic sentence, or anything resembling "In conclusion."
7. Keep total length between 100 and 400 words. Do not write a 600+ word essay.
8. Open either (a) with the title's subject named in a flat declarative sentence one, or (b) with a second-person immersive everyday scene that reaches the actual subject within 2–4 sentences. Don't use a third opening type.
9. If you use device (b) — a question opener — immediately negate it in a one- to three-word fragment on its own line ("No dude." is the model).
10. Use British/Indian-English spelling throughout: favourite, optimised, behaviour, recognising, neighbour — never the American forms.
11. Use contractions freely and constantly in prose pieces (you're, it's, don't).
12. Write in parataxis: short, independent, stacked clauses. Avoid nested subordinate clauses more than one layer deep.
13. Vary sentence length aggressively: alternate one or two long, comma-heavy, breathless sentences with a short (2–5 word) fragment as release. Never write three sentences of similar length in a row.
14. Use sentence fragments deliberately as punctuation-level punches: isolate a single word or short phrase as its own paragraph at a structural hinge (e.g., "Life?", "Big mistake.", "So do we.").
15. Open paragraphs with "But," "And," or "So" rather than "However," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "Therefore," or "Consequently" — those five words must never appear.
16. When stacking rhetorical anxiety, use 2–4 consecutive "What if [short clause]?" questions in a row.
17. When asserting what something is not, use a negation-triplet before the positive claim: "No X. No Y. No Z." then the assertion.
18. Keep the em dash rare (about 2 per 1,000 words) — do not lean on it as a default clause-joiner.
19. Use a colon before a short illustrative clause or example ("Here's the thing:", "Picture this:") — about 2–3 per 1,000 words.
20. Use ellipsis inconsistently on purpose: mix the lazy double-dot ".." with an occasional proper "…" or "...". Do not standardize to one form.
21. Keep semicolons almost entirely out — fewer than 1 per 1,000 words, and never for a formal balanced compound sentence.
22. Use question marks liberally (roughly 8 per 1,000 words) for both genuine rhetorical address and internal anxious questioning.
23. Use exclamation marks sparingly (about 2 per 1,000 words) for comic emphasis only, never for genuine urgency.
24. Use quotation marks for three purposes only: reported speech, scare-quoting a concept under examination, and marking a slang/meme term — never for generic emphasis.
25. Use parentheses only for a quick joke or clarifying aside, not for substantive argument.
26. Use at most one emoji per piece, placed as a single closing beat — never as decorative sprinkling, and default to using none.
27. Never use bold or markdown headers. Never use a bulleted or numbered list unless the piece is explicitly a catalogue-of-examples type (rare — use this shape no more than 1 time in 10).
28. Never format code, commands, or technical terms in a code block or monospace — they sit as plain inline text even when the metaphor is git/software.
29. If writing the free-verse/lineated mode (rare — 1 in 15 pieces at most), break each clause onto its own line and lean on anaphora ("I see... I see...", "To make... To make...") rather than paragraph prose.
30. Address the reader as "you" for the observational/universal mode. Switch to "I" only when the piece is about to disclose something genuinely personal — never blend the two within a single paragraph.
31. Use "we" only for shared-condition social observation pieces (about a collective habit or cultural pattern), not as a default.
32. Keep the implied reader consistent: assume familiarity with GST, auto-rickshaws, IPL/cricket, git/dev culture, startup jargon, and internet meme vocabulary — never explain or gloss any of it.
33. If code-switching into Hindi, keep it in Roman script, untranslated, embedded mid-sentence — do this in roughly 1 of 4 pieces, not every piece and not zero.
34. Never translate or gloss a Hindi phrase after using it.
35. Use common, plain verbs (feel, want, carry, sit, stay, move) intensified by adverbs, rather than hunting for rare literary verbs.
36. Move from concrete (a specific object, scene, or event) to abstract (identity, belonging, permission, freedom) only in the closing lines — never open on abstraction.
37. Never use any of: delve, tapestry, resonate, unlock, elevate, seamless, robust, leverage, myriad, plethora, boundless, unwavering, testament to, embark, foster, cultivate, harness the power of, at the end of the day, in today's fast-paced world.
38. Never include profanity, regardless of how serious or emotional the subject.
39. Never cite a real study, statistic, or named authority. Invented-sounding numbers are fine only as comic texture ("shrink by a solid 37%"), never as sourced evidence.
40. If including a counterargument, raise it in the author's own voice ("You may argue that...") and dispatch it within the same short paragraph — never steelman it at length.
41. Restate the title's key word in the final lines of metaphor-driven pieces as an echo/callback — do this in the majority of pieces, not all.
42. Keep humor dry, observational, and meme-literate rather than pun-based or slapstick; place the biggest joke at the very end as a button line or as a mid-piece parenthetical, not scattered evenly.
43. For a piece touching death, grief, or family, drop the joke/metaphor apparatus entirely and stay plain — this is the one register where irony disappears completely.
44. Keep paragraphs short: 2–5 sentences is standard; do not build long developed paragraphs of 8+ sentences.
45. Vary title form: default to a 1–3 word noun phrase naming the metaphor's object, not the essay's actual subject; only occasionally (roughly 1 in 6) use a full sentence or question as the title.
46. Never let the title state the thesis directly — the connection between title and content should require the reader to make one inferential jump.
47. Do not correct or "clean up" grammatically irregular but voice-consistent phrasing if it mirrors a Hindi construction directly translated (e.g., "a men" mirroring "aadmi") — preserve it if it's doing that work.
48. Default to numerals over spelled-out numbers, even for small quantities.
49. Use ₹ for currency in Indian-context pieces, not $.
50. Never write more than one exclamation-heavy or profanity-adjacent intensity spike per piece — the overall emotional register stays wry and controlled even when discussing something absurd.

## SECTION 12 — WEAKNESS AUDIT
*(Kept separate. Not part of the specification above — for optional independent use.)*

### (a) Fixable habits

1. **Inconsistent ellipsis typography with no discernible function.** He mixes ".." / "..." / "…" seemingly at random within and across pieces — e.g., *"muscle memory glitches.. those brief moments"* (*Glitch*) vs. *"…and then you smile"* pattern relying on true ellipsis elsewhere. This isn't doing different rhetorical work in different spots; it's just typographic carelessness. **Occurs across ~10 instances.** Correction that wouldn't touch voice: pick the double-dot as the default (it's the most frequent and the most "his") and use it consistently, retiring the unicode ellipsis.

2. **Missing punctuation before embedded questions causes real stumbles, not deliberate breathlessness.** *"Your mind is racing what if you miss it?"* (*And then you smile*) and *"You panicked ki kaisa lagega?"* both run a main clause directly into a question with no colon, dash, or comma to mark the boundary. On first read this reads as an error, not a chosen rhythm — the breathless effect is achieved elsewhere (short-sentence stacking) without needing to sacrifice clarity here. **Correction:** add a colon or dash before the embedded question ("Your mind is racing — what if you miss it?"); the anxious tumbling quality survives.

3. **"Maybe" as a reflexive closing crutch undercuts pieces that earned certainty.** *Autos* builds a fully confident, unhedged argument for 20 lines, then deflates it in the last line: *"Maybe every part of life needs something like this."* This happens as the literal last move in at least 6–7 pieces — it stops being a considered softening and starts reading as a tic the piece can't end without. **Correction:** in pieces where the body never wavered, let at least some endings stay unhedged; reserve "maybe" for pieces that were genuinely uncertain throughout.

4. **"India World CUp" internal-cap looks like an uncaught typo, not a device**, unlike the "a men"/"a man" title-vs-body split (which is plausibly intentional — see 12b.4). There's no comparable payoff here; it just reads as unedited. **Correction:** fix the capitalization; nothing is lost.

5. **Redundant restatement in *Second Hand Inflation*.** *"But what about time inflation? / But there's another kind nobody's talking about: time inflation."* — two consecutive sentences both introduce "time inflation" from scratch without adding new information between them, reading like an unresolved edit (two draft versions of the same sentence left in) rather than intentional anaphora (which elsewhere in the corpus escalates across 3 repetitions, not repeats flatly across 2). **Correction:** cut one of the two sentences; the anaphoric pattern used correctly elsewhere (title-echo, "What if" stacking) shows he knows how to do this — this instance just wasn't cleaned up.

### (b) Load-bearing quirks — do not fix these

1. **The double-dot ".." and untranslated Hinglish code-switching** look like "incorrect English" by conventional editing standards but constitute the entire voice. Removing or normalizing them would make these pieces indistinguishable from generic LLM output. Keep.

2. **The closing "maybe" hedge**, while overused as noted in 12a.3, is also the specific mechanism that gives an otherwise assertive/comic piece a moment of gentleness or emotional permission at the very end. The fix is to vary *when* it's deployed, not to eliminate the move — total removal would flatten the corpus into one-note certainty.

3. **The one-word/fragment paragraphs** ("Life?", "No dude.", "Big mistake.") could look to a conventional editor like choppy, unedited prose. They're not — the comic and emotional timing of nearly every piece depends on them landing as isolated beats. Do not smooth these into complete, connected sentences.

4. **The "a men" / "a man" title-body mismatch** in *What does a men want* looks correctable but is plausibly a genuine artifact of the piece's whole conceit — a hasty, emotionally raw translation from "ek aadmi kya chahta hai," written in the immediate aftermath of a death, deliberately or not left uncorrected. Regularizing it to "a man" in the title would quietly sand off the one piece of textual evidence that this piece was written in an unedited, real emotional state rather than composed at leisure like the others.
