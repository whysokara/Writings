I do like new things, just not the way they expect me to. I want a new T-shirt to feel like it has already been worn, like my body knows the fabric before my mind has to learn it. When something feels unfamiliar on my skin, I stay aware of it the whole time, and that awareness is uncomfortable. This is not limited to clothes. It shows up with phones, laptops, vehicles, even houses.

I do not enjoy unboxing. The plastic, the shine, the careful peeling. I would rather take things out of my cupboard, the way you reach for something that has always lived there. Over time that motion becomes muscle memory, and once muscle memory kicks in, I stop thinking. That is the state I like being in. I do enjoy the long battery life of a new phone or laptop, the smoothness, the efficiency, but do not expect me to treat the charger like a new white bedsheet. I want to use things, not tiptoe around them.

New objects quietly change your behaviour. You place them gently. You store them properly. You do not casually throw your laptop on the bed. You become careful, and I do not enjoy being careful with objects. The same discomfort exists in a new home. Fresh walls look beautiful, but how do you live in a space where you are constantly conscious of not scratching them? The first mark on the wall almost feels like permission, like the house finally agrees to let you live inside it.

Sometimes I think the problem is not that things are new, but that they are still aware of themselves. New objects carry a sense of presence. They sit upright, untouched, slightly alert. They notice how you place them, how carefully you move around them. And until that awareness fades, I stay aware too. Over time, they relax. A T-shirt forgets its fold lines. A phone stops shining. A wall accepts its first mark. The moment an object forgets that it is new, I forget to be careful. That is when it stops feeling owned and starts feeling lived with.
