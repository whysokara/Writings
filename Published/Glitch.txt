Every morning, you open your office laptop, fingers instinctively moving to type in your password. You don’t think about it, you just do it. Until one day, you don’t. Your fingers hesitate. A blank screen stares back, and suddenly, your brain feels like an empty notepad. You try a few combinations, still nothing. For five long seconds, you are lost, as if the part of you responsible for this task has momentarily stepped away.

I call these muscle memory glitches.. those brief moments when our autopilot mysteriously switches off. They pop up in the most unexpected places. a professional pianist who has played the same concerto hundreds of times, suddenly hesitating for a split second between notes. or consider the moment when you’re typing an email, and midway through, a simple word like necessary or business looks completely wrong. You rewrite it twice, thrice, still doubting yourself, even though you've spelled it a hundred times before. It’s not forgetfulness. It’s just a five-second glitch, a tiny lapse in the otherwise smooth operation of muscle memory. 

The phenomenon is particularly striking in the world of technology. Every developer knows that feeling when common terminal commands suddenly become foreign. You're about to push your code, fingers poised to type git push origin main, and suddenly blank!! Was it origin main or main origin? Your hands, which usually move faster than your conscious mind, freeze in hesitation. It’s as if our brain’s RAM needs a quick reboot.

But what if it’s more than that?

What if, in those fleeting seconds, you weren’t the same you? What if, somewhere in the tangled threads of the universe, an alternate version of you? one who took a different job, lived a different life, made a different set of choices briefly crossed over?

Maybe these lapses aren’t just lapses. Maybe they are echoes of parallel existences, where for a fraction of a second, the line between realities blurs. Maybe your muscle memory slips because, for those five seconds, it’s not your muscle memory at all. It belongs to another version of you one who has never typed this password, never pushed this code, never driven this route home.
It’s not memory loss. It’s not forgetfulness. It’s something else.
Maybe a tiny crack in reality. A brief shift between parallel lives.
Then, just like that, it’s gone. Your fingers remember. Your brain catches up. 
Everything is normal again.
Or is it?
